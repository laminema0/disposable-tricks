# Draw Every Monster's Verb Into Its Outline

The silhouettes stop looking elementary when each creature passes a pure-black fill test with one dominant mass, mixed straight and curved edges, tapered limbs, and detail clustered in one zone. Every monster becomes readable when its outline and idle motion show its single attack verb, and when that verb plays out as a visible wind-up, a short strike and an exposed recovery. Valve's discipline is the model: Team Fortress 2 classes had to be identifiable "even when viewed only in silhouette with no internal shading at all," and Left 4 Dead's survivors were redesigned because playtesters confused them with zombies ([Mitchell et al.](https://www.advances.realtimerendering.com/s2007/Mitchell-IllustrativeRenderingInTF2(Siggraph07%20Course%20Notes).pdf); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/in-depth-how-valve-makes-art-to-enhance-gameplay)). In a dark game, light does most of the communicating. Monster eyes and gaze cones carry alert state, a rising glow means an attack is charging, and the hero's own rim light replaces a visibility meter, as in Mark of the Ninja ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-mark-of-the-ninja-i-s-five-stealth-design-rules)). The flying monster should be a diver with no projectile. It hovers, freezes, flashes, dives in a straight line at where the hero was, then sits stuck on the ground where he can hit it. For the code side, bake each canvas-drawn silhouette into a shared texture at 2–3x size. Animate it with transforms, springs and point chains, and light the scene with a darkness RenderTexture plus additive glow sprites rather than Light2D. Most numeric values below are design starting points, not published standards, and are labelled as such. Treat them as the first setting for playtesting.

## The fill test separates professional silhouettes from clip-art

Valve's rule is the baseline. Moby Francke called the silhouette "a building block of character design," and Valve checked concept art by filling each character solid black. It also drew interior folds to repeat the outline's shapes, so the inside reinforced the outside ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/in-depth-how-valve-makes-art-to-enhance-gameplay); [Mitchell et al.](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall07/cos597B/papers/mitchell-team-fortress.pdf)). Limbo and Ori were built the same way. Arnt Jensen's concept trailer was praised for "strong silhouettes, fluid animation," and Ori's prototype "was just black-and-white silhouettes" ([Wikipedia, Limbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limbo_(video_game)); [Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/?p=4339)). The practical test for this game is to render each creature in pure black at its in-game height (roughly 60–150 px) on mid-grey and on the real dark background. If you cannot name the creature and its role, the outline is wrong, and adding interior detail will not fix it.

The usual shape-language convention is **circles = friendly, squares = stable, triangles = dangerous**. It is a working convention from tutorial sources, not proven psychology, but it gives a useful role contrast: low and blocky for a tank, sharp and angled for a fast enemy, round and upright for support. The differences should be visible "even in greybox" ([RocketBrush](https://rocketbrush.com/blog/shape-language-in-game-character-design-how-to-make-characters-readable-and-consistent)). The same sources name the main way designs end up looking elementary: building a figure from only one primitive. Straights and zigzags add tension, curves add looseness, and both are needed ([DonCorgi](https://doncorgi.com/blog/character-design-mistakes/)). Small stray gaps between limbs pull the eye without earning it. The fix is to move the limb or open the gap wide ([Lynda/LinkedIn](https://origin-www.lynda.com/3D-Animation-tutorials/Pushing-pose/2811343/2923304-4.html)). Valve also kept fine detail in one zone, the upper chest, instead of spreading it evenly, and used rim highlights rather than dark outlines to separate figures from the background ([Mitchell et al.](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall07/cos597B/papers/mitchell-team-fortress.pdf)).

Turned into rules that code can enforce, this gives eight checks. None is a published number. **(1) Big-medium-small:** one dominant mass at about 55–70% of the silhouette area, one secondary at 20–30%, and one or two accents at 5–10% each. Never make two masses nearly equal. The tutorial source for this hierarchy is thin ([Maya Beginner primer](https://mayabeginner.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Design/L3_DesignPrinicplesPrimer.html)). **(2) Edge variety:** every outline mixes long cubic-Bezier curves, short straight runs and one or two sharp corners. A shape made only of arcs reads as a primitive. **(3) Taper:** every limb, horn, tail, stem and cape tip goes from thick to thin to a point, drawn as a closed polygon whose half-width shrinks along its length. Never use constant-width strokes. **(4) Overlap:** shapes overlap (head in front of body, near leg over torso). Shapes that only touch read as a snowman. **(5) Negative space:** each gap is either larger than about 8% of body height or closed completely, never a 1–3 px sliver. **(6) Line of action:** hang the masses on one curved Bezier spine. Lean it forward for chasers and back for cautious idles, and keep both idle and profile poses asymmetric. **(7) Deliberate cut-outs:** an eye, a gap between ribs or a torn wing membrane, punched out with even-odd fill or `destination-out`, gives a second read inside the black. **(8) Shared grammar:** the same taper style, corner sharpness and eye treatment on every creature, so the cast looks like one world. Inconsistent proportions across a cast break that cohesion ([DonCorgi](https://doncorgi.com/blog/character-design-mistakes/)).

## A round, lit hero against wrongly proportioned threats

The appeal of the hero rests on stronger evidence than most of this report. Lorenz's *Kindchenschema* (a relatively large head, large eyes set below the skull's midline, round cheeks) raises cuteness ratings and caretaking motivation ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3596402); [Glocker et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3260535/)). **Avatars built with these traits were rated cuter, more likable and more approachable** than mature avatars, so the effect carries over to designed characters ([AI & Society 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01878-3/fulltext.html)). The game's big-headed, one-eyed hero already starts from this. The design decisions that remain are proportion and restraint.

Two studios give direct design reasons. Team Cherry's Ari Gibson described the Knight as "the simplest way of representing something bug-like" and noted that "putting a little cloak on a character is good because you can't see their arms" ([MCV/UK](https://mcvuk.com/when-we-made-hollow-knight/)). Moon Studios kept Ori tiny so "you can see further ahead, and have more time to react," and to show "the insignificance of the character" in a huge world ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/?p=4339)). Dark games also share one appeal trick: a single point of light or colour on the hero. Limbo's boy is identified by his white glowing eyes, Inside's boy wears the only red in a grey world, and the Knight's mask is the brightest thing on screen ([Giant Bomb review](https://giantbomb.com/user-reviews/id/121580/visual-artistry-and-well-designed-puzzles-collide-); [GameRevolution](https://gamerevolution.com/review/70543-inside-review); [Mechanics of Magic](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/?p=15853)).

Concrete spec, inferred from these sources. Make the head about **45–55% of total height** (2–2.5 heads tall), with stubby or hidden legs. Place the single eye below the head's horizontal midline and slightly toward the facing direction, about 25–35% of head width. Cut it out of the black as a light shape so it reads on a dark field, and leave a large forehead area above it. The cape hides the arms and trails longer on the back side, lagging the body as secondary motion. **The flower is the hero's one saturated or emissive element**, on a thin curved stem: it bobs, droops when hurt and perks up when safe. In a dried-out world that also makes it the story. **The sword is the only long straight line on the hero.** It sets up the one deliberate contrast with his roundness and marks the line every enemy will have more of. Keep him small on screen, as with Ori, so threats arrive early and the scale gap supplies the drama. Limbo's "behemoth spider" against a tiny boy and Little Nightmares' Six in giant rooms work on the same principle ([Wikipedia, Limbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limbo_(video_game)); [Wikipedia, Little Nightmares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Nightmares)).

## Each enemy owns one verb, one lane and one three-beat timeline

The design order matters as much as the drawing. Cuphead's team often decided how a boss plays before how it looks, and indie developers describe the same chain: "player moveset informs enemy behavior. Enemy behavior informs that enemies GFX" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/cuphead-developers-talk-cut-bosses-and-whats-in-the-cup-1820126327); [itch.io devlog](https://itch.io/post/8596185)). Mike Stout frames every attack as a question asked through a "small delay" that says "Okay, here I come" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/enemy-attacks-and-telegraphing)). Critpoints adds three rules that shape this whole roster. Attacks need clear startup, active and cooldown phases. Enemies must never "checkmate" the player. Different enemies should control different parts of the battlefield. A fast chaser also needs a limit, such as slow turning or a long recovery ([critpoints](https://critpoints.net/2016/10/28/what-makes-an-enemy-well-designed/)). Shape has to carry state, because colour alone fails under pressure: Fatshark gives Darktide enemies a readability priority and adds back attachments so long-range elites stand out in silhouette ([Darktide dev blog](https://devtrackers.gg/darktide/p/67a2496a-darktide-101-enemy-character-art-dev-blog)).

So each monster gets one verb and a **telegraph → active → recovery** beat. The recovery is the punish window and the moment its weak point opens, following the Mega Man Met pattern of a helmet that opens only to shoot. Suggested starting timings are 0.3–0.5 s telegraphs for basic enemies, 0.6–0.9 s for heavy attacks, 1 s or more for boss supers, and 0.4–0.8 s recovery. These are industry convention and were not verified against a source. They fit Jonathan Cooper's advice that enemy wind-ups should run longer than the player's so they can be read ([Game Developer / Cooper](https://gamedeveloper.com/production/the-12-principles-of-animation-in-video-games)).

| Role | Lane it controls | Silhouette | Idle motion (previews the verb) | Telegraph | Strike | Recovery / weak point |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Low charger** | Ground lane | Wedge 1.5–2.5x wider than tall, forward-pointing horn or snout, low centre of mass | Twitchy pacing, pawing, 0.1 s turns | Stops, squashes back (scaleY 0.8, scaleX 1.15), front edge or eye flares, scrape sound, ~0.4–0.5 s | Straight dash at fixed speed, cannot turn | Skids and wobbles ~0.6 s with its back exposed |
| **Rooted spitter** | A fixed zone | Vertical; roots fan into the ground; bulb on a curved stalk; no legs | Slow breathing pulse (scale 1.0–1.05 over ~2 s); bulb tracks the hero | Bulb swells 1 to 1.3, glow brightens, turns to aim, "inhale" sound, ~0.5–0.6 s | One slow glowing projectile on a clear line or arc | Mouth stays open and bulb deflates, ~0.5 s |
| **Flying diver** | Air space above the hero | Horizontal with spread wings (large gaps under them), sharp downward beak or talons, **no mouth or sac** | Sine bob plus wing flap | Stops bobbing, rises slightly, flashes, folds into a narrow arrow, ~0.5 s | Straight dive to the hero's position when the telegraph began (no homing) | Grounded and stuck ~0.7 s, **within reach** |
| **Tall stalker** | Reach radius | 2–3x hero height, arms reaching the ground, small or eyeless head, short or hidden legs | Slow heavy steps, arms sway with lag, groping sweep | Arm draws back high, claw tip glows, optional faint reach arc on the ground, ~0.6–0.8 s | Fast arc swipe with a visible trail | Arm drags, torso bends forward and exposes the head, ~0.8 s |
| **One-eyed watcher** | Sight cone | Mostly eye: a circle like the hero's, inverted, with a long triangular beam | Slow beam sweep, occasional blinks | 5 s exposure countdown shown on the beam itself (see below) | Alarm or lock-on | The eye, hittable when it blinks or closes after a sweep |
| **Boss** | Whole arena | 4–8x hero height, round core plus many points, a clear "head" read | Combines the roster's idles | Borrowed roster telegraphs, each with its own pose and sound | 3–4 moves from the regular enemies | Phase breaks: silhouette cracks open, eye revealed |

The **stalker** applies the clearest finding from the monster-design sources. Little Nightmares' Janitor is unsettling because his arms are "far too long for its body," his legs too short and his head too big, and because he sweeps the floor with his arms like someone feeling their way in the dark ([Monster Masterclass](https://residentanna.substack.com/p/monster-masterclass-little-nightmares)). Wrong proportions do more than spikes to signal threat.

**What the flying monster actually does.** Make it a diver, and keep any shooting flyer a separate creature with a visibly different silhouette. Classic flyer archetypes are predictable wave-path flyers such as Medusa Heads and homing divers such as the Ninja Gaiden birds ([critpoints](https://critpoints.net/2016/10/28/what-makes-an-enemy-well-designed/)). Developers point out that a flyer the player can never reach fails as an enemy ([itch.io devlog](https://itch.io/devlog/624502/game-design-enemies-design-what-i-learned.amp)). The dive solves both problems. It turns a hovering threat into a readable lunge, and its grounded recovery brings the flyer within sword range. If a shooting variant is ever needed, give it a glowing sac or eye on its underside, have it hover at a fixed height and drop slow projectiles. **Never let one flyer both dive and shoot**, or the player cannot tell which question is being asked.

**Merged monsters** keep each parent's most readable part (the charger's wedge base plus the spitter's bulb, for example) and alternate their verbs instead of firing them together, so each telegraph stays separate. The seam or join pulses as the weak point. The **boss** is the final exam of the bestiary. Its moves reuse telegraphs the player already knows. Phase changes need a clear beat: pause, brief invulnerability, flash, a visible silhouette change and an arena change, then faster telegraphs (about 20% faster as a starting point), never removed ones. Hollow Knight's Grimm is the usual example of a fair hard boss because each attack has a clear animation and a dodge window ([Choost Games](https://choostgames.com/blog/what-makes-a-good-boss-fight/)). Phases should change the arena as well as the boss ([Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/crafting-legendary-boss-battles)).

**Player-requested creatures** fit the same grammar once each gets a verb. A **dragon** is a long S-curve spine with triangular wings, built as an argonaut-style point chain. A **spider** is a small round body on eight long, thin, jointed legs, a Limbo-scale threat whose verb (lunge, web-drop) gets its own wind-up. A **zombie** is a human-like rectangle with broken asymmetry: a dropped shoulder, one arm forward, a forward lean. Its slow, lurching gait makes it a slow chaser by critpoints' rule. When a request does not name a verb, the maker should map it to the closest existing role so the telegraph vocabulary stays consistent.

## Eyes and gaze cones replace the stealth HUD

Mark of the Ninja is the best 2D precedent. It has no visibility meter: the hero is drawn black with red highlights when hidden and in full colour when lit. Guards have three readable awareness levels with entry cues, a glimpse at the edge of vision makes a guard investigate without going on full alert, and a ghost marker shows where the hero was last seen. The design rule is "transparent AI": players should immediately understand what triggered a change in behaviour. Sound is drawn as a spreading ring that "says exactly how far that noise will reach" ([Game Developer, five rules](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-mark-of-the-ninja-i-s-five-stealth-design-rules); [Game Developer, Reinventing stealth](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/reinventing-stealth-in-2d-with-i-mark-of-the-ninja-i-)). Klei also limited encounters to about one screen plus half to three-quarters of a screen in any direction, because larger 2D spaces disorient players ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-mark-of-the-ninja-i-s-five-stealth-design-rules)). One rule is easy to break: **the drawn cone must match the real detection area**. Invisible Inc players filed a bug when the rendered guard cone was larger than the actual detection zone ([Klei forums](https://forums.kleientertainment.com/forums/topic/41104-graphics-rendered-guard-vision-cone-of-wrong-size/)). Grace windows make being spotted recoverable, as with MGSV's Reflex Mode and Aragami's slow-motion reaction ([Metal Gear Informer](https://www.metalgearinformer.com/new-metal-gear-solid-v-info-and-screens-from-the-la-studio-opening-event/); [Aragami reviews](https://www.trueachievements.com/game/Aragami-Shadow-Edition/reviews)).

For this game, draw each monster's gaze as a two-tier wedge from its eye. The bright inner core detects fast, and a faint outer "glimpse" band builds suspicion slowly. Clip the wedge against rocks, pillars and grass with a visibility polygon, so the shape on screen is exactly the shape the AI uses ([Feronato](https://emanueleferonato.com/?p=12908); [phaser-raycaster](https://github.com/wiserim/phaser-raycaster)). When the hero ducks into grass, the cone visibly stops at the grass tips, with a slightly brighter lip along the cut edge. The ladder below is a proposed design. Its timings are not sourced and need tuning in playtest.

| State | Eyes | Cone | Body | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Steady soft cool glow, slow blink | Soft, alpha ~0.15–0.25, sways with head | Idle walk | Low hum or breath |
| Suspicious | Brighten and narrow | Tightens and lengthens toward the stimulus; fills over ~0.6–1.5 s | Head turn, pause, lean in | Rising tone |
| Alerted | Bloom pop, then danger colour | Snaps to full brightness | Rears up or freezes ~0.3–0.5 s (grace), then acts | Sharp sting |
| Searching | Hot, slow pulse | Sweeps at a "ghost" hero silhouette at last-seen spot | Walks there, looks both ways | Sniffing motif |
| Giving up | Cools over ~1 s | Fades back to soft | Settle or shrug | Falling tone; patrols with a slightly wider cone |

Colour temperature is the state axis: cool when calm, amber when suspicious, a hot danger hue when alerted. **Every colour step must be paired with a shape or motion change** (cone width, eye narrowing, pulse rate), because the Xbox Accessibility Guidelines say not to convey information by colour alone, and red against green commonly fails ([Xbox AG 102](https://devdocs.xbox.com/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/102)).

**The watcher's 5-second countdown should live in the world, not in a number.** Over the five seconds the beam narrows and brightens toward a hot core. A ring of light around the eye closes like a clock face, and dust motes in the beam speed up. A pulse and a sound tick mark the final second, and a single "commit" flash marks the lock. Breaking line of sight visibly resets it. That keeps the same "charging means brighter and tighter" rule every other monster uses. MGSV's directional, cone-shaped lens flare is the precedent for using light itself as the warning ([Metal Gear Informer](https://www.metalgearinformer.com/?p=9380)). The hero carries his own exposure state. Hidden means pure black with a faint cool rim, so the player never loses him even when the monsters do. Exposed means a warm rim and an eye glint.

## Light is the telegraph in a dark silhouette world

Silhouette games stay readable by keeping values limited. In Limbo, light and shadow define the playfield and the direction of travel ([Girls on Games](https://www.girlsongames.ca/2017/04/27/art-examination-playdead-limbo/)). Valve used rim light so "the shadowed side" doesn't get "lost into the background," with shadows going cool and "never going black" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/in-depth-how-valve-makes-art-to-enhance-gameplay)). Inside's renderers used dithering so subtle gradients were not "drowned in colour-banding" ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023002/Low-Complexity-High-Fidelity-INSIDE)). For the desert, keep the foreground silhouettes darkest. Make each background parallax layer lighter and less saturated toward the horizon, place 2–3 slowly drifting haze bands between layers, and keep the deepest darkness out of the middle band where the hero and monsters move. A black silhouette gets its contrast from what is behind it. The Xbox guideline asks for **3:1 contrast for large elements and 4.5:1 for standard ones, measured against the lowest-contrast part of the background**, and 7:1 in a high-contrast mode ([Xbox AG 102](https://devdocs.xbox.com/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/102)). Give the cone edges a crisp 1–2 px lip at the true detection boundary, because a gradient that fades to zero leaves the edge unclear.

Light also becomes the attack grammar, with three parts. **Charge = gather:** additive particles stream inward to the eye or mouth while the halo shrinks and brightens, following Stout's charge-up pattern of particles gathering, a reverse "vacuum" sound, then a flash ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/enemy-attacks-and-telegraphing)). **Commit = one universal eye flare:** scale 1 to 2.5 and alpha 1 to 0 over about 120 ms, the same for every monster. **Danger colour is reserved:** one hot hue family appears only on imminent danger (alerted eyes, charging glow, projectiles). Lamps stay warm amber, calm monster gaze stays cool, and collectibles never share the monster hue. Projectiles get a white core, a coloured halo, a 3–6 ghost trail and a muzzle flash at the source. They also light up the rocks they pass. Players report that matching player and enemy bullet colours makes them hard to tell apart ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/16632227)). For area attacks in side view, light the strip of ground where the attack will land.

Hit and death feedback should stay in proportion. One set of defaults is a 0.08 s hit flash, weak hit-stop of 0–0.03 s, strong 0.05–0.08 s and critical 0.1–0.15 s. The same source warns "make everything flashy, and everything becomes ordinary," saves shake for finishing hits and flashes only the target, never the full screen ([uhiyama-lab](https://uhiyama-lab.com/en/notes/unity/unity-game-feel-hit-feedback/)). Because the project's aim is to steady mood, not intensify it, keep shake low-amplitude, lerp state colours instead of strobing, keep grace windows generous, and ship reduce-flash and visibility-boost options. A silhouette death works as freeze, white flash, squash, then 8–15 fading shards. Celeste uses a particle burst for a Seeker's permanent death ([Celeste wiki](https://celeste.ink/wiki/Seeker)).

## Springs, chains and paper dolls bring the silhouettes to life

Cooper's reading of the animation principles for games points to the cheapest sources of appeal. **Squash and stretch with volume kept constant, follow-through and overlap, easing, arcs, and anticipation**, with short wind-ups for the hero and longer ones for enemies ([Game Developer / Cooper](https://gamedeveloper.com/production/the-12-principles-of-animation-in-video-games)). Rain World shows these can come from code instead of frames. Joar Jakobsson builds creatures from "a bunch of points in space" joined at fixed distances, with "paper doll" sprites placed on top every frame. The slugcat is just two circles locked at a fixed distance, with its limbs and tail as "cosmetic additions drawn on top" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/crafting-the-complex-chaotic-ecosystem-of-i-rain-world-i-); [Unity blog](https://unity.com/en/blog/exploring-procedural-design-rain-world)).

Five tools cover almost every case, each a few dozen lines of TypeScript. **Springs** handle secondary motion. Daniel Holden's exact critically damped spring, parameterised by a half-life, stays stable at any frame time ([The Orange Duck](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)). t3ssel8r's second-order system adds frequency, damping and an initial-response value, where negative response gives free anticipation and values above 1 give overshoot ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/tag/game-design)). Its commonly reproduced constants were not verified against the original video. **Point chains** drive tails, the dragon's spine and tentacles. In argonaut's lizard, a 14-joint spine uses 64 px links and a 22.5° bend limit, and the body outline comes from per-vertebra widths ([argonautcode](https://github.com/argonautcode/animal-proc-anim)). **Verlet sticks** suit capes: cloth often needs only one relaxation iteration ([Jakobsen](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/advanced-character-physics)). **Two-bone IK with step thresholds** handles legs. argonaut replants a foot when its target drifts past a threshold, a Godot spider steps diagonal pairs in turn, and Godwalker lets legs lag visibly during fast moves so a dash reads as a dash ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/ik-driven-procedural-spider-locomotion-in-godot-4-5); [Hirshfield](https://joshhirshfield.substack.com/p/procedural-animation-techniques-in)). **Sine oscillators** give breathing.

Per-creature starting recipes, all inferred. **Hero:** scaleY 1 ± 0.03 breathing over 1.5–3 s with scaleX moving opposite, the head lagging by a 0.15–0.3 rad phase, the flower on a bouncy spring (damping about 0.4), and the cape as a Verlet strip or Rope pinned at the shoulders. **Walk cycles** drive phase from distance travelled, not time, so feet don't slide. **Wings** flap fast down and slower up, with cos-foreshortened scaleY. **Spider** steps at a threshold of 0.6–1.0x leg reach, takes 80–150 ms per step with an arc height of 20–30% of the step. **Stalker arms** are chains with long spring half-lives (150–300 ms), so they drag and sway. Give every instance a random phase and about ±10% period variation so groups never move in lockstep. To animate a single maker-generated silhouette, cut it along 1–4 lines into parts, store a pivot at each cut, and put a rotation spring on each part. Even a body plus a lagging head is a visible improvement.

## Bake once, animate transforms, and fake light in Phaser 3.90

Pin `phaser@3.90.x`. Phaser 4 renamed FX to filters and made `setTintFill` a no-op ([v4 reference](https://unpkg.com/phaser@4.2.1/skills/v4-new-features/references/REFERENCE.md)). **Draw each silhouette once into a CanvasTexture** with the browser's anti-aliased 2D context, at 2–3x display size. Use Path2D, `bezierCurveTo`, even-odd cut-outs and a 1–1.5 px lighter rim stroke on the light-facing edge clipped to the shape. Call `refresh()`, then display the result as an Image scaled to 1/S. A live `Graphics` object recalculates its whole contents every frame, and WebGL1 framebuffers can't be anti-aliased ([Textures docs](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/textures); [Discourse](https://phaser.discourse.group/t/is-generatetexture-culled-by-the-viewport-camera/7167/4); [RenderTexture docs](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/gameobjects/render-texture)). Draw every body part into regions of one shared canvas and register them as frames, so all monsters batch into one texture. Keep pages within about 2048 px for mobile, and use power-of-two sizes if you enable `mipmapFilter` for small sprites ([mipmapFilter](https://newdocs.phaser.io/docs/3.80.0/focus/Phaser.Renderer.WebGL.WebGLRenderer-mipmapFilter)).

**Skip Light2D.** It cannot light Graphics or Shapes, it needs normal maps, and it defaults to 10 lights ([LightsPlugin API](https://docs.phaser.io/api-documentation/3.88.2/class/gameobjects-lightsplugin); [Lights concepts](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/gameobjects/light)). Instead, use one screen-sized darkness RenderTexture. Each frame, `fill` it near-black at about 0.85 alpha, then `stamp` a soft radial `lightBlob` with `erase: true` at every lamp, flower, firefly and eye, inside a `beginDraw`/`endDraw` batch. Then lay ADD-blend tinted copies of the blob on top for coloured warmth ([RenderTexture docs](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/gameobjects/render-texture)). Group the ADD objects together, because every blend-mode change flushes the WebGL batch ([BlendMode API](https://docs.phaser.io/api-documentation/3.90.0/namespace/gameobjects-components-blendmode)). `PointLight` is a cheap alternative for torch blobs. Add a faint tiled noise overlay at about 2–4% alpha to break up banding.

Effects follow a budget. **Hit flash is `setTintFill(0xffffff)` for 60–90 ms, then `clearTint()`**, and tints are effectively free ([Tint docs](https://docs.phaser.io/api-documentation/3.90.0/namespace/gameobjects-components-tint)). preFX `addGlow` (with `setPadding`) gives the hero a rim, or a boss a charge glow when you tween `outerStrength` from 0 to 6. Each FX is an extra render pass, so keep per-object FX to the hero and one or two key objects at a time. Put vignette or grading once on `cameras.main.postFX`, which the changelog calls "much faster" than applying it per object ([3.60 FX changelog](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/phaser@4.1.0/changelog/v3/3.60/FX.md)). Containers cannot take preFX, and their cost grows with nesting depth, so keep monsters one level deep ([Container docs](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/gameobjects/container)). `Rope` handles tails, capes and tentacles: update `points[i]` from a chain, call `setDirty()`, and fade the tip with per-point alphas ([Rope docs](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/gameobjects/rope)). Script telegraphs with `this.add.timeline` (`at:0` crouch, `at:350` flash, `at:420` strike plus a small shake, `at:600` clear). Use `emitter.explode` for spit and impacts with capped `maxParticles` ([Time docs](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/time); [Particles docs](https://docs.phaser.io/phaser/concepts/gameobjects/particles)). Camera shake defaults to 0.05 intensity, which is violent on a phone, so start near 0.006 ([Camera API](https://docs.phaser.io/api-documentation/3.88.2/class/cameras-scene2d-camera)). Update every spring and chain in one `update` loop with dt clamped to 1/30, skip cosmetic solves for off-screen monsters, and pool enemies with Groups. As a starting point, 30–60 monsters of 5–8 atlas parts each is reasonable after Phaser 3.60's mobile batching rework ([3.60 Mobile Performance](https://unpkg.com/phaser@4.2.1/changelog/v3/3.60/MobilePerformance.md)), but no published benchmark confirms these counts, so profile on a mid-range phone.

## Conclusion

The common thread is that a monster's outline, idle motion and light should all describe the same verb before it acts. The charger is a wedge because it charges in a straight line. The stalker's arms are wrong because its threat is reach. The watcher is mostly eye because watching is its attack. With that rule, the player-requested creatures stop being a risk to readability: each request maps to an existing verb and inherits its telegraph, its lane and its recovery window. The maker can enforce this as data, with each creature declaring a role, and the role setting proportions, edge sharpness, idle and wind-up timing. That keeps a growing, user-generated bestiary consistent.

The second lesson is that light does more work in the dark than drawing does. Eyes, cones, charge glow and the flower are the most legible elements on screen and the cheapest to change in code. The flower is the hero's identity, the eyes are each monster's state, and the reserved danger hue is a promise to the player. The weakest evidence is in the numbers. Wind-up lengths, suspicion fill rates, contrast thresholds for black-on-dark sprites and Phaser performance budgets all lack published sources. They should be the first things tested in playtest, with the reduce-flash and visibility options built in from the start.
